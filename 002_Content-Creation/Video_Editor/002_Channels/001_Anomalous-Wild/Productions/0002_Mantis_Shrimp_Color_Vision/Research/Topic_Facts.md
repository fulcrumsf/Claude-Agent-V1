Subject type: species-level (mantis shrimp / stomatopods generally, with peacock mantis shrimp — Odontodactylus scyllarus — as the "smasher"-type specific example for the punch mechanic).

## Color Vision
- Mantis shrimp eyes contain up to 12–16 physiologically distinct photoreceptor classes (vs. 4 in humans), spanning UV through far-red — genuinely hyperspectral vision.
- Each photoreceptor is tuned to detect one specific narrow wavelength band. Unlike human vision (which compares signals across a few broadband receptors to interpolate color), stomatopods appear to use a serial/temporal scanning approach — scanning their independently-rotating eyes across an object and sampling each of the 12 color channels roughly in sequence, rather than combining them simultaneously.
- They can also detect 6 types of polarized light: horizontal, vertical, two diagonal linear types, and two circular polarization types — a sense with no human equivalent at all.
- Counterintuitively, despite having far more color receptor types than humans, behavioral testing shows mantis shrimp are comparatively poor at discriminating between close wavelengths — the "more receptors ≠ better discrimination" surprise is one of the most narratively useful hooks in this research.
- Each eye is independently mobile (like a chameleon's) and itself has trinocular vision (depth perception using a single eye) due to three distinct regions scanning the same point.

## Punch Mechanic (smasher-type stomatopods, e.g. peacock mantis shrimp)
- The strike uses a saddle-shaped exoskeletal spring, not raw muscle speed. Muscles contract slowly (hundreds of milliseconds) to load the spring; a latch holds it cocked until flexor muscles release it.
- Strike acceleration: ~10,000 × g. Club velocity: up to 23 m/s (~51 mph) underwater. Full strike duration: under 80 microseconds — about 50× faster than a human eye blink.
- Cavitation: the appendage moves water so fast that local pressure drops below vapor pressure, forming a collapsing vapor bubble. The bubble's collapse produces its own shockwave, heat flash, and secondary force — meaning prey/targets are hit twice: once by the physical club, once by the imploding cavitation bubble microseconds later.
- This is the mechanism that inspired the "shrimp can punch through aquarium glass" popular claim — a plausible/mostly-true framing worth a caveat rather than an outright statement (their strike force is measured in the range that can crack standard glass, not a folklore exaggeration).

## Notable/Surprising Details (good hooks)
- Despite the see-more-colors-than-us hyperspectral eye, their brains do very little of the receptor-blending work human brains do — they may effectively "know" a color the instant a single receptor fires, trading discrimination precision for speed.
- The punch is one of the fastest strikes in the animal kingdom by acceleration, comparable in order of magnitude to a .22 caliber bullet's muzzle energy at the point of impact (popular-science framing, not a literal ballistics equivalence).
- Species-level story, not a specific individual — no named/famous individual mantis shrimp exists in the public record the way "Coco the Gorilla" does.
