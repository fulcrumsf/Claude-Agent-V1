## scene_01
This animal sees colors that do not have names.
Not because no one has looked. Because human eyes are not built to receive them.

## scene_02
Human eyes carry three kinds of color receptor.
Every color you have ever seen was built from those three.
The mantis shrimp carries up to sixteen.

## scene_03
Remarkably, its eyes do not stop at color.
Each one is mounted on its own independently moving stalk, aimed in a different direction, processing a different part of the world at the same time.

## scene_04
What's extraordinary is that this system should not work as well as it does.
Science has only recently discovered why an animal would need to see this much of the color spectrum in the first place.
We'll come back to that — but first, there is a stranger version of this eye.

## scene_05
Most animals, including humans, see light only as color.
The mantis shrimp also sees the angle light waves twist through — a property called polarization, invisible to nearly every other eye on Earth.
Of all the creatures on Earth, only a small handful can detect this directly. The mantis shrimp is the only one known to detect circular polarization — a twisting pattern of light that does not occur naturally anywhere else in the animal kingdom.

## scene_05b
Researchers believe some mantis shrimp use this hidden channel to signal to each other in a code no predator can read, because no predator's eyes can see it.

## scene_06
But the eyes are not even the most anomalous part of this animal.
What they are built to track is.

## scene_07
The mantis shrimp's strike is one of the fastest movements in the animal kingdom, accelerating so quickly it boils the water in front of it for a fraction of a second, producing a flash of light from the collapsing bubble alone.
And its eyes — the same sixteen-receptor, polarization-reading eyes — are fast enough to track the entire event in real time, while every other eye nearby only sees a blur.

## scene_08
An eye built to see a spectrum with no name, watching a strike too fast for anything else to follow.
What we now know is that this animal was never just hiding in a burrow. It was watching everything, in colors none of us will ever see.
