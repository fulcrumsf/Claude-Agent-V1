# Feedback Loop — 2026-08-29

## Grandma-and-Bear Shot-Idea Calibration

Tony rejected the latest batch of ten ideas as insufficiently grounded in what a real YouTube witness would plausibly capture. The ideas focused on random object interactions instead of a believable setup, visible action, and natural human reaction.

Key corrections:

- Do not describe an object as stolen if the shot needs that object to be visible; define the visible setup and action clearly.
- Check scale before proposing an interaction. A bear cannot realistically nap inside an ordinary laundry basket.
- Do not force animal behavior into human behavior, such as waiting for permission or carrying objects like a person, unless the behavior is genuinely plausible and clearly staged by the camera perspective.
- Avoid arbitrary props with no believable reason to be outdoors, such as an abandoned birthday cake.
- Avoid generic or low-stakes actions that would not make a compelling captured moment, such as dragging a welcome mat.
- The humor should come from a believable human treating an extraordinary bear encounter as ordinary, not from invented punchlines or random object gags.
- A potentially workable premise needs a specific, observable setup and a natural reaction. Example: a bear blocks a grandmother using a walker, and she angrily tells it to move.
- “Grandma finds a bear eating birdseed in the shed” may work because the location, food source, and discovery are plausible; it still requires a clear visible setup.
- “Bear sitting in a porch rocking chair” is borderline but potentially usable because the visual is immediately understandable and the absurdity comes from the bear occupying a familiar human space.

Process lesson: before presenting shot ideas, run both a physical plausibility check and a human-captured-video check: would the camera realistically be present, would the setup naturally exist, can the viewer understand the action immediately, and is the unusual behavior plausible enough to feel like a real outlier rather than a constructed gag?

Further clarification: the surprise-interaction formula itself is not the problem. The problem is vague implementation. A shot idea must be described as a filmable sequence, not merely as a premise. For example, the laundry-basket idea needs to specify the neighbor's phone-camera perspective, Grandma hanging clothes with the basket nearby, the bear entering frame, taking the basket a short distance while remaining visible, attempting to sit in it, and the physically believable result such as crushing or partially collapsing it. The explanation must make the humor, scale, camera perspective, and escalation visible enough for Tony to evaluate before a prompt is written.

The bear should behave like a bear; the human's ordinary, context-appropriate reaction is where the absurdity can land. Avoid “commercial comedic” lines that make the scene feel written instead of captured.

Important correction: Grandma returning is not automatically a payoff. It was being repeated as a mechanical second beat rather than because it improved the joke. A scene may end on the bear's visual behavior or on one simple human reaction when that is already the complete humorous moment.

Shot-idea simplification rule: avoid adding entrances, exits, props, or reaction beats that do not improve the core visual. Prefer off-frame entrances and existing environmental cover when they reduce physics and continuity burden without hiding the important action.

New reference pattern from Tony: a grainy backyard night-security camera sees three bear cubs climb onto a trampoline and gradually become more enthusiastic about bouncing. Lights arrive off-frame, suggesting Grandma has come home without requiring a visible car; the lights turn off, Grandma enters through the fenced gate, climbs the existing trampoline steps, and starts jumping with the cubs. This is the desired type of outlandish scenario: the camera setup is believable, the animal behavior is visually legible, the escalation is sequential, the environment supports the action, and the ending is a surprising human choice rather than a forced verbal punchline.

Anti-slop correction: do not reuse the same Grandma-returns pattern, same “bear creates an inconvenience” structure, or same quiet reaction ending across ideas. Variation should include different emotional attitudes, escalation shapes, camera sources, environments, group sizes, and endings while preserving clear cause-and-effect.

Purple Elephant principle: use an unusual, attention-stopping visual that is still grounded in a believable capture context and coherent escalation. Do not interpret this as random weirdness or a literal humor formula; the surprise must remain understandable and physically plausible enough to feel like a real outlier.

Repetition correction: do not keep defaulting to gates, sprinklers, or Grandma repeatedly coming back outside. These became low-effort generation templates rather than purposeful story beats. Before proposing another idea, compare its environment, camera source, action, escalation, human role, and ending against recent ideas; reject it when the structure is repeating without a strong reason.

Species/country variation rule: international settings and less-familiar bear species may appear as occasional outliers, roughly two or three per compilation when appropriate, but should not become the organizing pattern unless the episode explicitly calls for a global theme. Exclude sloth bears from general bear brainstorming because the name may make viewers expect a conventional sloth rather than a bear.

Approval interpretation rule: when Tony approves or passes an idea, treat the approval as validation of the whole scene's logic, believability, humor, and overall picture—not as approval of its setting, action, camera, dialogue, or other ingredients as a reusable recipe. Extract the underlying judgment, then generate a materially different scene that must pass its own holistic review.

Believability nuance: believable filming circumstances do not require an event to be common or statistically normal. A rare event can pass when it is technically plausible within the location, species/ecology, culture, weather, physics, and camera opportunity, and when the filmer could realistically notice and capture it. “Uncommon but plausible” is valid; “technically possible” by itself is not sufficient if the scene lacks supporting context or contains contradictions. The Russian passenger filming the unusual bear-in-a-convertible sighting is the model example.

Pink Elephant examples: the Russian bear-in-a-convertible sighting, Grandma jumping on the trampoline with bear cubs, and the bear riding away in the canoe are strong examples of plausible absurdity. Their value is that they are so visually extraordinary that viewers want to watch, while the setting and physical progression still allow the event to feel like a rare real recording. Use this as a high-impact category within the remarkable-but-believable principle, not as a recipe to repeat.

Latest concept calibration: “Grandma” means an older-woman/grandma figure, but she must plausibly belong in the environment and occupation. A rural fire-station scene can work if the woman is clearly a janitor or cleaner, not a firefighter. Keep physical quantities and outcomes realistic, such as only four or five pears falling from a shaken branch. Match the reaction to the filming context: a woman inside a camper closes the camper door, not a tent zipper. A polar-bear bus-stop scene works better when a passenger films an older woman and polar bear sharing the bench, then watches the woman board while the bear remains seated. A dashcam crosswalk scene is stronger when the bear appears to escort an elderly woman across the road, creating understated absurdity through protective behavior rather than dialogue.

Consolidation rule: when a previously accepted idea receives a better revision, count the revised form as the same unique concept rather than counting both versions. The sprinkler concept is one example: replace the earlier “bear tests the sprinkler while Grandma closes the door” version with the simpler “bear enters and plays in the spray” version rather than counting both.

Concept-specification rule: before a concept is considered ready for generation, define its filming device, camera movement or locked position, environment and geometry, time and lighting, tone, subject motivation, physical action order, and natural endpoint. The specification should make the entire visual flow understandable without requiring a long prose explanation.
