# Robotto Gato — Channel Strategy & Execution Brief
# This plan was created with OpenAI Codex. 
> **Purpose:** A working business plan for building Robotto Gato, plus a reusable prompt for turning the strategy into an actionable project plan in Claude Code.

## 1. The idea

**Robotto Gato is a practical AI creator-tech channel.** It reviews AI software, teaches specific workflows such as building a video pipeline, and documents the creator’s real experience using these tools to make things. The channel helps curious creators understand how the pieces fit together so they can make informed choices and take a first step of their own.

The point of view is **learn by building, explain what actually happened**. Show the setup, decisions, costs, useful results, limitations, and revisions—not just polished outcomes or tool lists. A viewer should get value even if they never buy a tool or monetize a channel.

### Positioning

**For creators who want to use AI to make better content and build repeatable workflows, Robotto Gato offers honest software reviews and hands-on walkthroughs grounded in real projects.**

**Working promise:** “I test the tools and build the workflow in public, so you can see what works, what doesn’t, and how to adapt it.”

Avoid claims of easy or guaranteed income. The authority comes from showing your work, explaining tradeoffs, and updating advice when experience changes it.

## 2. Audience and value

### Primary audience

- Curious creators, solo operators, and aspiring YouTubers who want to use AI without buying every new tool.
- People who need a concrete result—such as a repeatable short-form video pipeline—not another abstract list of tools.
- Beginners who benefit from seeing the process, decisions, and mistakes explained in plain language.

### What viewers should gain

1. A clearer understanding of which tools solve which problems.
2. A workflow they can follow or adapt, with realistic requirements and costs.
3. Confidence to take a small first step and make something.
4. Useful references, checklists, prompts, and templates to continue on their own.

## 3. Content system

Build around three connected pillars. Every video should be searchable, shareable, or both, and should answer one specific viewer question or document one meaningful experiment.

| Pillar | What it covers | Example angles |
|---|---|---|
| **AI software, tested** | Reviews, comparisons, setup, pricing/value, limitations, and alternatives | “I used [tool] for a week”; “[Tool A] vs [Tool B] for this task”; “What the free plan can actually do” |
| **Creator workflows** | Step-by-step builds for specific outcomes and the systems connecting tools | “Build an AI video pipeline from idea to published Short”; “Turn one script into several formats”; “What to automate—and what to keep manual” |
| **Build in public** | Experiments, behind-the-scenes decisions, failures, revisions, and lessons | “What I learned making my first [project]”; “The tools I stopped paying for”; “I tried this workflow so you don’t have to” |

### Editorial rules

- Start with the viewer’s task or question, then show the relevant tools.
- Demonstrate the workflow on a real example; include settings, prompts, time, and costs when useful.
- Distinguish firsthand testing from features or claims you have not verified.
- Include tradeoffs, failure points, and who a recommendation is not for.
- Use affiliate links only when relevant, clearly disclose the relationship, and keep the recommendation useful without a purchase.
- End with one achievable next step and a question that invites viewers to share what they are building.

### Repeatable video shape

1. **Outcome and stakes:** What are we trying to make or decide?
2. **Context:** Who this is for and what is needed.
3. **Build/test:** Show the actual process, including important choices.
4. **Result:** What worked, what failed, and what it cost in time or money.
5. **Takeaway:** A reusable workflow, decision rule, or next step.
6. **Relevant resource:** Link to the tools, checklist, guide, or follow-up video; disclose affiliate links where applicable.

## 4. Publishing approach

Start with a pace that can be sustained alongside learning and building. A reasonable starting experiment is **one useful long-form video each week or every other week**, with a few short clips from each recording where practical. Treat this as a test, not a permanent quota.

For the first 90 days, prioritize a coherent body of work over frequent pivots:

- **Weeks 1–2 — Set the foundation:** Define the visual/verbal basics, channel description, disclosure language, simple recording/editing setup, and a backlog of viewer questions. Pick one workflow to build.
- **Weeks 3–6 — Publish the first series:** Make videos that introduce the workflow, review the key tools in context, and show a complete first build. Capture reusable screenshots, prompts, and notes as you work.
- **Weeks 7–10 — Improve from evidence:** Review comments, retention, click-through, and production effort. Make follow-ups that answer real questions; compare alternatives or repair weak parts of the workflow.
- **Weeks 11–13 — Package a useful resource:** Turn a proven workflow into a free checklist or small downloadable worksheet. Ask viewers what would help them apply it. Do not build a large course before the audience and repeated demand are clearer.

Build each larger topic as a small series: a use-case tutorial, a relevant tool review or comparison, and a reflection/update. This gives viewers multiple ways in and lets the channel learn from one project rather than constantly starting over.

## 5. Monetization ladder

Monetization should follow demonstrated usefulness. Keep the early focus on publishing, learning the audience, and building trust; add each revenue stream when it naturally fits the content and capacity.

1. **Affiliate links — early, selectively.** Link to software actually used in the video. Make disclosures clear in the video and description. Track clicks and conversions, but do not let commission rates determine the recommendation.
2. **Clipping-style UGC / tech campaigns — after a portfolio exists.** Save examples of clear demos and product explainers. Create a small media/UGC portfolio and define deliverables, usage rights, and rates before pitching or accepting campaigns. Keep sponsored work clearly labeled and opinions honest.
3. **Amazon Associates — alongside gear reviews.** Review gear that is genuinely part of the creator setup (for example, microphones, lights, storage, or cameras). Disclose the relationship and follow the program’s current rules before publishing links.
4. **Downloadable guides with worksheets and checklists — once a workflow is proven.** Sell the process, decision points, examples, and reusable worksheets—not a promise of income. Test a focused guide at a fair price; improve it using buyer questions and feedback.
5. **Prompt and template packs — as companion assets.** Make prompts editable and explain when and why to use them. Include examples and limitations so buyers learn a method rather than receive a mystery bundle.
6. **Skool community and/or course — later.** Consider this after viewers repeatedly ask for structured help, feedback, accountability, or deeper instruction. Validate the curriculum with a small pilot before investing in a large course or ongoing community commitment.

The offers should form a useful path: **free videos → free checklist → focused paid guide/template → structured course or community**. Viewers should be able to learn from the channel without purchasing.

## 6. What to measure

Use metrics as clues, not as the purpose of the work.

- **Discovery:** impressions, click-through rate, search traffic, and which titles/topics bring in new viewers.
- **Usefulness and trust:** average view duration, returning viewers, substantive comments/questions, and whether people report completing the workflow.
- **Sustainability:** time to research, build, record, edit, and publish each piece.
- **Business signals:** relevant affiliate clicks/conversions, resource signups/downloads, guide sales, campaign inquiries, and repeat requests for deeper instruction.

Review monthly. Keep topics that attract the right viewers and are enjoyable or feasible to make; revise topics that get clicks but do not hold attention or deliver a clear outcome.

## 7. First decisions and open questions

Make lightweight choices and revise from experience; the strategy does not depend on having every answer now.

- Which first project will anchor the launch series (a video pipeline is a strong candidate)?
- What is the realistic publishing cadence given available time and current production skills?
- Which creator type should the first 5–10 videos serve most directly?
- What channel voice and visual identity make “Robotto Gato” recognizable without slowing production?
- Which tools are already available, and what is the initial monthly software/gear budget?
